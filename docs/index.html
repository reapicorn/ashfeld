Overview

Ashfeld is a lab that puts you inside a fictional industrial city and gives you the keys. The backends are real, the protocols are live, and the data is already there waiting.

Nobody agrees on who is in charge. Nobody agrees on who still works here.

hollowcrown

The Bureau — citizen records. The only list anyone agrees to use. A web UI for clerks, an API for systems.

darkhorn

Six companies, six protocols. HTTP REST, JDBC, LDAP, SFTP, SOAP, and MQ — same thirteen operations, six different doors.

ironhold

The vault beneath the city. Privileged credentials, checkout policies, rotation schedules — the keys nobody is supposed to have.

thorngate

The gate that decides who gets in and under what conditions. Lifecycle, roles, provisioning policies — governance at the infrastructure layer.

Hollowcrown

Hollowcrown was the name of the council that governed Ashfeld before it fell apart. When the council dissolved, the name passed to the bureau that kept its records — the registry of workers, departments, appointments and departures.

It is the source of truth for identity in the city. The web UI gives humans a way to manage that record. The API gives systems a way to read it.

What the Bureau records, the backends are expected to mirror. A new citizen joining the city means a new entry in the system. A termination means the opposite.

Terminated citizens are never deleted. Removing a name from the roster was considered worse than keeping a wrong one.

Darkhorn

Darkhorn is the southern district of Ashfeld, built around the old processing yards and the warehouses that outlasted them. The companies that moved in after the foundries closed brought their own ways of doing things — their own ledgers, their own couriers, their own gates.

None of it was designed to connect. It just grew, one system at a time, until untangling it became someone else's problem.

HTTP REST JDBC LDAP SFTP SOAP AMQP / MQ

Every company handles the same three things — who works here, what they can access, and whether they're still active. The paperwork is identical. The process is not.

Ironhold

Below the city, past the service corridors and the locked sub-levels, there is a room that nobody officially acknowledges. The vault. Where the real keys live — service passwords, admin credentials, the codes for the systems that keep everything else running.

Ironhold is the layer that governs access to those keys. Who can check them out. How long they can hold them. Whether the lock changes when they give them back.

Most of the city pretends it doesn't exist. The people who know about it spend a lot of time arguing about who should have access to it.

Privileged credentials are not just stored — they are managed. Checkout, approval, automatic rotation. The vault decides when a key expires, not the person holding it.

Thorngate

At the eastern edge of Ashfeld, where the old administrative district meets the newer infrastructure, stands a gate that was never meant to be a checkpoint. It became one anyway.

Thorngate is the governance layer — the engine that decides who belongs where, what they are allowed to do, and what happens when that changes. It does not manage individual systems. It manages the rules that all systems are supposed to follow.

It does not move quickly. It was not designed to. The city's access decisions move through it like permits through a bureaucracy — correctly, eventually.

Thorngate does not approve requests. It enforces policies. The difference matters when you are the one waiting.
